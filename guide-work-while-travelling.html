Working While You Travel

The realistic ways backpackers fund long trips — what works, what pays, and the rules you must not break.

Almost every long-term traveller hits the same moment: the savings are going down faster than planned, and the trip is far from finished. The good news is that there are real, legal ways to keep going. The bad news is that most "get paid to travel" advice online skips the boring but important parts — skills, visas and taxes. This guide covers both.

The golden rule: working in a country usually needs the right visa or work permit. A tourist visa almost never allows local paid work. Breaking this can mean fines, deportation or a ban. Always check the official immigration website of the country you are in.

1. Teaching English

Teaching English is still one of the most reliable ways to live abroad for longer. Demand is strong across much of Asia, Latin America and parts of Europe.

  • What you need: usually a TEFL/TESOL certificate (120 hours is the common standard), and often a degree for a full work visa. Native-level English helps, but many schools hire fluent non-native speakers too.
  • How it works: schools and language centres normally sponsor the work permit. Contracts often run for a school year, so this suits people who want to stay in one place for a while.
  • Online teaching: teaching online gives you more freedom to move, but pay depends heavily on your own students and reputation.

Tip: summer and holiday camps are a great way to try teaching for a few weeks before committing to a full contract.

2. Remote and freelance work

If you can do your job with a laptop — writing, design, translation, web development, video editing, customer service, social media or sales — you may be able to work for clients back home while you travel.

  • Start building clients before you leave. Finding your first client from a hostel with bad Wi-Fi is hard.
  • Keep a simple portfolio website and a clear list of services and prices.
  • Remember time zones: a client in London and you in Vietnam means meetings early in the morning or late at night.
  • Check the rules: some countries now offer special digital nomad visas; others say remote work on a tourist visa is a grey area. Taxes usually still apply in your home country.

3. Work exchanges and volunteering

Work-exchange platforms connect travellers with hostels, farms, eco-projects and families who offer free accommodation (and sometimes food) in return for a few hours of help a day. It will not make you money, but it can cut your biggest cost — accommodation — to zero and help you meet locals.

  • Read reviews from previous volunteers carefully.
  • Agree hours and duties in writing before you arrive.
  • Be aware that some countries treat even unpaid work as work that needs a permit.

4. Seasonal and working-holiday jobs

Many countries run working holiday visa schemes for younger travellers (often aged 18–30 or 18–35, depending on your passport). These allow you to take ordinary jobs — bar work, fruit picking, ski seasons, hostel work, tourism — legally for up to a year or more. Check which countries have an agreement with your country, as places are sometimes limited.

5. Selling what you create

Some travellers build income from content: YouTube videos, blogs, photography, e-books or online courses. It is slow to start and rarely pays in the first year, but it can grow into something that pays while you sleep. Focus on being genuinely useful to a specific audience rather than chasing views.

A simple plan to get started

  1. Choose one main income option that matches your skills.
  2. Check the visa and tax rules for the countries on your route.
  3. Save a buffer of at least two to three months of costs.
  4. Build your first source of income before relying on it.
  5. Track your money every week so there are no surprises.
